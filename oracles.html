Oracles for AI Systems

On bounded trust between components, and an offer of help

uprootiny · November 2026

Abstract

A system built around a language model does not repeat itself: the same input can give a different output, and no output vouches for itself. Such a system needs parts it can ask and believe, within limits it knows. I build those parts and help teams decide where they belong. This note says what such a part is (§1), offers a retainer for three kinds of trouble (§2), shows the work behind the offer, two of it in public (§3), and ends with a way to talk (§5).

1Position

I build oracles for AI systems: parts the rest of a system can trust, within limits that are stated, measured and kept.

1.1What an oracle is

Origin. The word comes from computability theory and software testing; see note 1.

An oracle is a component that a system asks instead of computing the answer itself, and whose answer it accepts under a known contract.1 The contract says which questions it takes, how often it is right, what it costs, how long it takes, and what it does when it does not know.

Being an oracle is a role, not a kind of software. A test suite, a proof checker, a database, a smaller model, a reviewer on another team, a court: each can serve. What is inside may stay closed. It need not be right every time; it has to keep its contract.

1.2Bounded trust

I do not rebuild anyone’s AI from scratch. I make it possible for the parts of a system to rely on each other to a known degree, and I write the degree down. Four limits make it up:

  • Domain. Which questions the part accepts, and which it must refuse.
  • Reliability. How often it is right, and whether its stated confidence matches that rate (calibration2).
  • Cost and latency. What one answer costs in money, tokens and time.
  • Behaviour at the edge. What it returns when it does not know: an abstention, an escalation, or a wrong answer delivered with confidence.
Placement rule. Put an oracle where checking is cheaper than guessing, or where the truth is owned by someone outside the system.

The useful asymmetry is between predicting and checking. No general procedure can decide non-trivial properties of arbitrary programs (Rice’s theorem), but checking one proposed answer against a specification is often cheap. Most of the engineering in this field is placing checks where that asymmetry holds.

1.3Definitions

The working vocabulary, for readers who want it exact. Q is a set of questions, A a set of answers.

Oracle
O : Q → A, together with a contract on price, latency and reliability.
Verifier
V : (x, y) → accept | reject(reason). Worth having when checking y costs less than producing it.
Decision oracle
D : (state, questions) → a probability for each answer.
Witness
W : constraints → example | ⊥, where ⊥ means the constraints contradict each other.
Fast reject
A cheap filter that is sometimes wrong, placed in front of an expensive one that is rarely wrong.

1.3.1Cascades

Chain oracles cheapest first. If stage i costs ci and passes a case on to the next stage with probability pi, the expected cost of one case through three stages is

E[cost] = c₁ + p₁·c₂ + p₁·p₂·c₃

so most of the saving comes from making the early stages cheap and letting them hand on few cases.

2The offer

I work on retainer with teams that build and run such systems. The first conversation costs nothing; after it, both sides know whether a retainer makes sense.

2.1Three kinds of trouble

2.1.1A system on its last breath

It still runs, but every change is a risk and nobody is sure how long it will hold. I look at it with you and say how long it is likely to hold, what is holding it up, and what to do first.

2.1.2People on parts of one system

Several people or teams each own a part, and the parts no longer fit. I talk to each of them, find where their assumptions contradict one another, and make the shared work easier.

2.1.3AI that is costly and wrong

Typical tools. Verifiers, cascades with a fast reject in front, and explicit token budgets per loop (§1.3).

A model-based feature costs too much per answer and is wrong too often. I help make it cheaper and more often right by deciding what gets checked, by what, and in which order.

2.3Which offer fits

Scope. Four questions give a rough match, not a diagnosis. Nothing you choose here leaves your browser.

A short calibration bench. Answer four questions; the result updates as you go.

(a) What is closest to your situation?
(b) Who will make the changes?
(c) What form of help are you after?
(d) Can the team set aside a few hours a week to talk?

Result. Answer the questions above to see which offer, if any, is closest.

3.2Current work

In public. The first two are on GitHub under marchdown. Each has a one-command check, and neither claims more than its check shows.
  • A verifier for agent behaviour (tidy-harness, Swift). The input is an agent’s session log; the contract is a set of rules written as data; the output says, per rule, pass, fail or not applicable. Two independent implementations, a Swift core and a Babashka script, must agree exactly on real logs before a number is believed; today they agree on 151 sessions. A rule that does not apply says so instead of passing.
  • A reliability contract made measurable (free-llm-harness, Python). A routing layer over free models with bounded retries, a fallback chain and explicit success criteria: a maximum failure rate and a p95 latency. Probes test whether the contract holds. Over 88 requests: 100 % success on the quota probe, 83 % under an outage probe, p95 6.4 s. The numbers are the contract, not the wish.
  • Proofs as oracles. A model proposes; a proof checked by Lean3 accepts the result or reports the step where it fails. The checker is the oracle for the model’s output.
  • Short feedback loops. Small cycles of propose, check and correct that keep a system consistent with its own specification, each with a fixed token budget so spend stays bounded.

3.3Research interest

How attention behaves in sustained work with AI: how long it holds, how it moves, and how that differs between roles. I would like practitioners to share a common, tested picture of it, and I am open to a research position on the question.
